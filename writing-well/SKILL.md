# Writing Well

A skill for writing compelling nonfiction—blog posts, essays, and articles—based on principles that reliably produce work readers can't put down.

## The Core Framework

**Writing Quality = Novelty × Resonance**

People read nonfiction to learn and to feel. Your writing succeeds when it delivers both.

**Novelty** — Ideas that are new, significant, and not easily intuited:
- Counter-intuitive: "Oh, I never realized the world worked that way."
- Counter-narrative: "Wow, that's not how I was told the world worked!"
- Shock and awe: "That's crazy. I would have never believed it."
- Elegant articulations: "Beautiful. I couldn't have said it better myself."
- Make someone feel seen: "Yes! That's exactly how I feel!"

**Resonance** — Ideas that take root in readers' minds, wrapped in:
- Stories
- Analogies
- Examples
- Authentic voice

## 1. Choosing What to Write

The best topic is the one you can't *not* write about—the idea bouncing around your head that compels you to get to the bottom of it.

### Pair Objective with Motivation

**Objectives** (what you're trying to accomplish):
1. Open people's eyes by proving the status quo wrong
2. Share a solution to a tough problem
3. Distill an overwhelming topic into something approachable
4. Tell a suspenseful and emotional story that imparts a lesson
5. Articulate something everyone's thinking about but no one is saying
6. Identify key trends on a topic, then use them to predict the future
7. Contribute original insights to a field through research and experimentation

**Motivations** (why you'll see it through):
- Does writing this get something off your chest?
- Does it help reason through a nagging, unsolved problem?
- Does it persuade others to do something you believe is important?
- Do you obsess over the topic and want others to geek out over it too?

Your objective clarifies what you're trying to accomplish; your motivation ensures you actually finish.

## 2. Writing Introductions

An intro is the minimal information needed to:
1. Introduce the breadth of contents so readers can decide if it's for them
2. Hook readers into reading more

### Hooks: Half-Told Stories

A hook raises a question then teases only part of the answer:

**Narrative hook** — Share the beginning of a profound change, but withhold the conclusion:
> My clothes turned to ice. I took them off and looked for a fresh pair—before realizing I hadn't actually brought one. The pair I just removed took off with the wind and over the mountainside. I was now standing—bare—on an arctic summit. That was the day I lost everything. And this is the story of what happened next.

**Research hook** — Highlight fascinating findings, but only a portion:
> I tracked all 90 living individuals born without the ability to sense pain. 80 are living normal lives. The remaining 10 are defying everything we know about survival.

**Argument hook** — Present a bold claim but withhold how you arrived at it:
> There's a 90% chance Cloudtex goes bankrupt within thirty days. This post walks you through the corruption that triggered their downfall.

### Generating Hooks

1. Ask: "If someone else wrote my intro, what are the most captivating questions they could pose?"
2. Write those questions down, even if you lack the answers
3. Rank by how much they interest you
4. The top questions become your hooks—pose them in your intro, don't reveal answers

### Combat Skepticism

Readers push back. Proactively address these objections in your intro:

| Skepticism | Solution |
|------------|---------|
| **Superficial** — "I won't learn anything new" | Tease original insights |
| **Irrelevant** — "You won't cover what I care about" | List the points you'll cover |
| **Sloppy** — "This will be badly written" | Rewrite intro to be clear, succinct, intriguing |
| **Implausible** — "You won't deliver on hooks" | Include quotes from authorities |
| **Untrustworthy** — "You're not qualified" | Share credentials, or make hooks so captivating they can't help but continue |

### Get Feedback on Your Intro

Ask friends who represent your audience:
1. Rate the intro from 1-10 on interest (no 7s allowed—force a decision)
2. "What questions would you most want answered?"
3. "Don't be afraid of a low score—you spare me from wasting time on an article no one wants to read"

Rewrite until you reach 8/10.

## 3. Writing Your First Draft

### Speedrun the First Draft

Write a terrible first draft as quickly as possible. Use placeholders like `<to fill out>` whenever stuck. Goal: generate and connect ideas, not explain everything perfectly.

> "When you write a first draft, you write it for yourself. When you rewrite it, you write it for everyone else." —Stephen King

### Build Your Outline

Two types of points:
- **Supporting points** — What's needed to make your argument
- **Resulting points** — What happens when your argument is true

Supporting points typically come first; resulting points typically come last.

**Example outline for "Challenging the status quo":**
1. State the reader's current view is false *(supporting)*
2. Establish how so many people being wrong hurts our world *(supporting)*
3. Establish what's required to change views *(supporting)*
4. Predict how the world would be different after transition *(resulting)*
5. Explore exciting byproducts of that difference *(resulting)*

### Fill the Outline

Ideas bubble up from:
- **Hooks** — Answer captivating questions from intro
- **Experience** — Reflect on observations and anecdotes
- **Research** — Acquire knowledge
- **Experiments** — Run tests
- **Brainstorming** — Voice-record then transcribe
- **Mental models** — Think critically

Most ideas arrive *while* writing—not before. You write in order to think.

### Filter Ideas: Interesting & Surprising

**Interesting ideas** — Make your next point whatever interests *you* most. Skip boring parts. When something bores you, it probably bores readers too. You're a proxy for your audience.

**Surprising ideas** — Find information that contradicts what readers expect. Use Paul Graham's method: Learn the basics of a topic, then find new information that surprises even your knowledgeable self—it'll surprise laypeople too.

When stuck, ask:
1. How can I make this point more convincing?
2. What are the interesting implications of what I just said?

## 4. Rewriting

Great authors' first drafts are typically bad. They have the discipline to aggressively rewrite for:

1. Being clearly understood
2. Persuasive reasoning
3. Sustaining curiosity
4. Resonance (story, analogy, poetry)

> "The process of writing your second draft is the process of making it look like you knew what you were doing all along." —Neil Gaiman

### Goal 1: Clarity

Write sentences a thirteen-year-old could follow. They should understand the *logic*, not necessarily every detail.

**Remove abstract phrasing:**

Bad: "The obstacle facing media organizations is charting an economically sustainable course through a landscape of commodity journalism."

Good: "News companies are struggling to stay in business because anyone with a Twitter account can report the news now."

**Use fewer ideas per sentence.** Longer sentences mean more details held in memory—harder to understand.

**Provide examples:**
- Good and bad examples (before-and-after)
- Make examples fun and topical
- Complex examples might become diagrams

### Goal 2: Succinctness

Succinctness is the percentage of significant thoughts out of all thoughts communicated. A 50,000-word book can be succinct if dense with insights.

**Three-step process:**

1. **Rewrite sections from memory** — Take a break, then rewrite focusing only on critical points. Fluff falls away.

2. **Remove unnecessary detail** — Delete filler words that don't add necessary context. Rephrase what remains.

3. **Rephrase paragraphs from scratch** — Now that you know what you want to say, say it in fewer words.

**The Tweet test:** Try compressing your post into a single tweet. If you can without losing anything important, publish the tweet instead.

### Goal 3: Intrigue

**Technique 1: Novelty** — Counter-intuitive, counter-narrative, surprising, or elegantly poetic claims.

**Technique 2: Withholding information** — Choose which answers to reserve until the end.

**Dopamine counting:**
1. Ask feedback-givers to highlight every sentence that gives them a dopamine hit ("Ahh, that's cool/profound")
2. Mark each hit with a counter: (3)
3. Rewrite long stretches without hits—make them concise or inject more novelty

**The trifecta of intrigue:**
1. Captivating intro that buys goodwill
2. At least one peak of insight or surprise
3. Satisfying ending that justifies why it was worth reading

Not every paragraph has to be interesting. The peak-end rule: people judge experiences by their most intense point and their end.

## 5. Getting Feedback

The most efficient way to improve. Not optional.

**Ask for this feedback:**
> Read my article slowly and transcribe your reactions:
> 1. **Tell me what to delete** — "I'm drifting here. This isn't compelling."
> 2. **Tell me what to double down on** — "Dopamine hit. Go further in this direction."
> 3. **Tell me what isn't clear** — Then score 1-10 on satisfaction.

**Rules of thumb:**
- If you agree with feedback, implement it
- If 3/10 people have same feedback and you're ambivalent, incorporate if quick
- If 4+ have same feedback and you disagree, *you* are wrong—ask why

**Writing is done when:**
- You've fulfilled your topic objective
- You've hit 7.5+ feedback score
- You're no longer making significant edits

**Your future self is your best feedback source.** Stephen King shoves manuscripts in a drawer for six weeks. A week is often enough. Switching text editors tricks your brain into seeing work with fresh eyes.

## 6. Style

### Voice

Your voice isn't word choice—it's your unfiltered self reflected on paper:
- Tone of voice
- Sense of humor
- Eccentricities
- Viewpoints

Write like you talk to a lifelong friend. In early drafts, discard self-censoring. In later drafts, remove sensitive details.

**Inauthentic voice** happens when you:
- Absorb others' styles
- Project expertise you don't have
- "Write smart" with words you'd never use in conversation

Antidote: Record yourself talking, transcribe it, work from that.

### Vividness

Crank the saturation dial. Pick words that simply work better.

> "A tiny creature faced with a Himalaya-sized trek. Undeterred, it marches on, its legs as skinny as a runner's, up towards the summit above which a fog from the Atlantic hovers."

**Metaphor technique** — Ascribe human qualities to inanimate things:
- Angry storms
- Shrieking sirens
- Dancing leaves
- Coy flames

### Poetry

Find evocative, unconventional ways to say meaningful things. Describe effects, not causes.

**First-order:** "The day was hot."
**Second-order:** "The day melted our popsicles."
**Third-order:** "Even the bugs were looking for air conditioning."

Third-order requires imagination and puzzle-solving—the reader pieces it together.

> "Instead of telling us a thing was 'terrible,' describe it so that we'll be terrified." —C.S. Lewis

## 7. Overcoming Blockers

### Blocker: Lacking ideas

- **Trigger ideas that bug you** — Consume others' views, note when you strongly disagree
- **Capture real stories** — Write vulnerable stories you'd tell a close friend
- **Be a diligent note-taker** — Save every interesting/surprising idea
- **Explain concepts to others** — Teach, mentor, call friends

### Blocker: Fear no one will read

Write to clarify your own thinking. Readership is a bonus. This mindset is critical for a long writing career. Truly great, authentic content eventually surfaces.

### Blocker: Fear of judgment

- Add disclaimer: "I'm sharing early thoughts. Encourage readers to share experiences."
- Start with curation, weave in original thoughts over time

Have a bit of shamelessness about being wrong in public. Too much = charlatan. Too little = never publish.

### Blocker: Procrastination

**Avoid distractions:**
- Listen to atmospheric music (steady beat, no vocals)
- Disconnect WiFi, leave phone in another room

**Speed past tedium:**
- Only write sections that immediately interest you
- Write out of order
- Start with speed—momentum carries you forward

If procrastinating for months, you may not be sufficiently passionate about the topic.

---

## 8. Practice & Mastery

**Turn writing into a game:**
- How young of a reader can understand my nuanced argument?
- How many unnecessary words can I delete?
- How much of a page-turner can I make this?
- How beautiful can I make my prose?

**Dissect good writing:**
- Highlight best and worst parts of favorite posts
- Identify what makes them so
- Writers who post sporadically often have more to say than those on fixed schedules

## Quick Reference Cheatsheet

**Process:**
1. Choose topic
2. Write intro with hooks, brainstorm talking points
3. Get intro feedback (aim for 8/10)
4. Create outline (supporting + resulting points)
5. Write messy first draft
6. Rewrite for clarity, succinctness, intrigue
7. Cycle: rewrite, rest, receive feedback
8. Copy edit for grammar, word choice, flow

**Clarity:** Write for 13-year-olds. Simple wording, one idea per sentence. Use examples.

**Succinctness:** Rewrite sections from memory. Remove unnecessary words. Rephrase from scratch.

**Intrigue:** Hook + peak + satisfying ending. Dopamine counting. Withhold information.

**Feedback:** Aim for 7.5+ score. Take breaks to see with fresh eyes. If 4+ people say the same thing, listen.

**Style:** Authentic voice. Vivid details. Describe effects, not causes.
